Chapter 01

How Threats Get In

Hackers rarely "hack" in the Hollywood sense. Most attacks come down to one of four doors — and they're all about tricking you, not breaking technology.

  • Phishing emails & texts — A fake message tricks you into clicking a link or handing over a login. Still the #1 attack method worldwide.
  • Unpatched software — Outdated apps and operating systems have known holes. Attackers exploit them automatically, at scale.
  • Reused or weak passwords — One breached account becomes all your accounts when you use the same password everywhere.
  • Malicious downloads — Fake software, cracked apps, or "free" tools that silently install malware alongside whatever you wanted.
The hard truth: No security product defeats a user who clicks the wrong link. Awareness is your first layer of defense.

Chapter 02

Passwords & Multi-Factor Authentication

This is the single highest-impact thing you can do. Do both of these and you've eliminated the majority of account-takeover risk.

  • Use a password manager. Bitwarden (free, open-source) or 1Password. It generates a unique, random password for every account — you only remember one strong master password.
  • Turn on MFA everywhere it's offered — especially email, banking, and Microsoft 365. An authenticator app (Microsoft Authenticator, Authy) is stronger than SMS codes.
  • Use passkeys when available. Windows Hello, Face ID, and hardware keys can't be phished. They're the future — and they're here now.
  • Never reuse passwords. One breach turns into every account if you do. A password manager makes this effortless.
Free pick: Bitwarden is audited, trusted, and completely free for individuals. There's no excuse not to use one.

Chapter 03

Keep Everything Updated

Most malware attacks exploit vulnerabilities that were already patched — on systems where the update was never installed. Updates are security, not just features.

  • Windows Update: on, automatic. Don't postpone indefinitely. Apply updates within a week of release.
  • Your browser is your biggest attack surface. Chrome, Edge, and Firefox all auto-update — confirm they're actually doing it.
  • Don't forget apps. Adobe Reader, Zoom, Office, and media players — outdated versions are open doors.
  • Router firmware. Log into your router once a year and check for firmware updates. Most people never do this once.
  • Get off Windows 10. Microsoft ended support in October 2025. If you're still on it, you're receiving no security patches.

Chapter 04

Email & Phishing

Phishing is the #1 delivery method for ransomware, credential theft, and wire fraud. Learning what to look for takes two minutes.

  • Check the actual sender address — not the display name. "Microsoft Support" from support@random-1234.ru is not Microsoft.
  • Urgency = red flag. "Your account will be suspended in 24 hours" is almost always fake. Real companies give you time and options.
  • Hover before you click. On desktop, hover over any link to see the real URL before you click it.
  • Don't open unexpected attachments — even from people you know. Their account may have been compromised and used to send it.
  • When in doubt, go direct. Instead of clicking a link in an email, open your browser and navigate to the site yourself.
2026 update: AI-generated phishing now has perfect grammar and can reference real details about you scraped from social media. Typos no longer identify fakes.

Chapter 05

Router & Wi-Fi

Your router is the gateway to every device on your network. Most people configure it once and never touch it again — attackers count on that.

  • Change the default admin password. Every router ships with "admin/admin" or similar — it's publicly searchable online.
  • Use WPA3 if your router supports it. If not, WPA2-AES. Never WEP. Never open.
  • Create a guest network for visitors, smart TVs, and IoT devices. Keep untrusted devices off your main network.
  • Disable remote management unless you specifically need it — it's an open door from the internet.
  • Make your Wi-Fi password a passphrase. "BlueSky-Mango-Thunder" at 22 characters beats "P@ssw0rd8!" in every way.

Chapter 06

Backups

Ransomware doesn't care how good your antivirus is. Backups are your last line of defense — and the only recovery option that actually works.

  • Follow the 3-2-1 rule: 3 copies of your data, on 2 different media types, with 1 copy offsite or in the cloud.
  • Automate it. A backup you have to manually run is a backup that won't happen at the moment you need it most.
  • Test your restores. A backup you've never tested is just hope. Restore one file to confirm it actually works.
  • Cloud sync ≠ backup. If you delete a file in OneDrive or Google Drive and it syncs, your "backup" deleted it too. Use versioned backup instead.
For businesses: Modern ransomware finds and destroys connected backups before triggering encryption. You need at least one offline or immutable backup that attackers can't reach.

Chapter 07

AI Scams 2026

The threat landscape has fundamentally shifted. AI has made scams faster, cheaper, and far more convincing than anything we've seen before.

  • Voice cloning is real. Attackers can clone a voice from seconds of audio found online. A distressed "family member" call demanding gift cards or a wire transfer may not be who you think.
  • Real-time video deepfakes exist. Businesses have lost millions to fake "executive" video calls authorizing wire transfers. If something feels off, it probably is.
  • Create a family safe word. Agree on a private code word in advance. Anyone who can't produce it in an emergency call isn't who they say they are.
  • AI phishing looks professional. Spelling errors, grammar mistakes, and awkward phrasing no longer flag a scam. Every message can look polished now.
  • Verify out-of-band. If a request comes by email, verify by phone. If by phone, verify by text. Never confirm using the same channel the request arrived on.
For businesses: Implement a verbal verification protocol for any wire transfer, password reset, or unusual IT request. One extra call can prevent five-figure losses.
