Chapter 01

How You Actually Get Infected

Most attacks aren't sophisticated hacks — they're tricks. Infections happen one of three ways, and understanding them is half the battle.

  • You get tricked. This is the #1 method. Someone downloads and runs malware themselves — disguised as an invoice, a software update, a "fix" for a fake virus, or a cracked app. Being tricked has nothing to do with intelligence; it happens to smart people every day. The rule: only download software from the original company's official website, after searching for it yourself.
  • Your software is out of date. Criminals build pages that silently test your browser for known vulnerabilities — and if found, infect your computer automatically, without you clicking anything. These are called exploit kits. You don't have to visit shady sites; attackers inject them into legitimate websites via compromised ads. Keeping software updated is your primary defense.
  • A zero-day hits. Rare, but real: hackers discover a flaw before it's patched and use it silently. The defense is simple — never open attachments or office documents you weren't specifically expecting, and don't click links in texts or messaging apps from unknown sources.
The rule: Your antivirus saying something isn't a virus doesn't mean it's safe. Antivirus is the airbag — if it fires, something already went wrong. Don't rely on it alone.

Chapter 02

Windows Security

This is the complete setup. Done right, your machine becomes virtually impervious to viruses you don't actively try to install yourself.

  • Stay on Windows 11. Windows 10 support ended October 2025. If you're still on it, you're getting no security updates — period. Upgrade now or replace the machine.
  • Keep Windows Update current. Go to Settings > Windows Update and apply updates within a week of release. Don't skip them indefinitely.
  • UAC to full — always. Set User Account Control to "Always notify." Anything lower lets malware silently escalate to admin permissions. It's not bubblewrap — it stops real attacks.
  • Turn on BitLocker. If you have Windows Pro, enable BitLocker Drive Encryption. If you have Home, check Settings > System > About for Device Encryption. Without this, anyone who gets physical access to your machine can read all your files.
  • Use Chrome or Firefox — 64-bit, up to date. Make sure auto-update is on. Your browser is your biggest attack surface.
  • Install uBlock Origin. Add it to Chrome or Firefox from the official extension store. The majority of malware threats arrive through malicious advertisements — even on major websites. This stops them.
  • Windows Defender is good — use it. Enable cloud-based protection in Windows Security settings. You don't need a paid third-party antivirus; Defender is a legitimate, well-maintained product.
  • SmartScreen on. Search for SmartScreen in Start and ensure it's set to warn before running unrecognized apps. This stops most downloaded malware before it even runs.
Hard rule: Don't pirate software. Cracked apps and keygens are the easiest way for criminals to get people to willingly run malware. If you wouldn't unlock your front door for a stranger, don't run untrusted executables.

Chapter 03

Router & Wi-Fi

Your router is a small computer running its own operating system — and it needs the same care. Most people configure it once and never touch it again.

  • Update the firmware. Routers have bugs and vulnerabilities just like any computer. Log into your router admin page (usually 192.168.1.1 or 192.168.0.1), find firmware updates, and apply them. Do this once a year.
  • WPA3 or WPA2-AES only. In your router's Wi-Fi settings, set the security type to WPA3 if available, or WPA2-Personal + AES. Never WEP, never "open."
  • Make your Wi-Fi password a passphrase. At least 12 characters. You rarely have to type it, so make it long. "BlueSky-Mango-Thunder" beats "P@ssw0rd" in every way.
  • Change the default admin password. Every router ships with a known default password (often "admin"). Leaving it means any malware that infects a device on your network can take over your router. Write the new password on a piece of paper and tape it to the router.
  • Change your DNS to Quad9. Set your DNS servers to 9.9.9.9 (primary) and 149.112.112.112 (secondary). Quad9 blocks connections to known malicious domains and removes ISP ad hijacking. It's free and run by a nonprofit security consortium.
  • Turn off WPS. Wi-Fi Protected Setup is a poorly implemented feature with known vulnerabilities. Disable it unless you have a Wi-Fi printer that specifically requires it.
  • Turn off remote management. There's no reason for this to be accessible from the internet. It should be off by default but verify it.
  • Use a guest network for IoT devices. Smart TVs, thermostats, cameras, and other connected devices belong on a separate guest network — isolated from your computers and phones. Enable "guest isolation" so devices can't talk to each other.
Skip these — they don't help: SSID hiding and MAC address filtering look like security but do nothing against actual attackers. They only add setup friction for you. The only thing that works is a strong Wi-Fi password.

Chapter 04

Email & Phishing

Phishing is the delivery method for most ransomware, credential theft, and wire fraud. Learning what to look for takes two minutes and pays off for life.

  • Check the actual sender address — not the display name. "Microsoft Support" from alerts@random-domain.ru is not Microsoft. Click the sender name to see the real address.
  • Urgency is a red flag. "Your account will be locked in 24 hours" is a pressure tactic. Legitimate companies give you time and options. Slow down.
  • Hover before you click. On desktop, hover over any link to see the real URL in the status bar before clicking. If the destination doesn't match who supposedly sent it, don't click.
  • Don't open unexpected attachments. Even from people you know — their account may be compromised. If you weren't expecting a file and the email doesn't make sense in context, reply and ask before opening.
  • When in doubt, go direct. If you get an email from your bank, don't click the link. Open a new browser tab and go to your bank's website yourself. Always.
  • Check suspicious links before clicking. Copy the link and paste it into urlscan.io to see a screenshot of where it goes. Or paste it into VirusTotal to check against security blacklists.
2026 update: AI-generated phishing now has flawless grammar and can reference real personal details scraped from LinkedIn, social media, or data breaches. Typos and awkward phrasing no longer indicate a fake. Trust the link destination, not the writing quality.

Chapter 05

Scams to Avoid

Some "security" products and tactics are the scam, not the solution. Knowing what to ignore is just as valuable as knowing what to do.

  • Registry cleaners are fake. Programs like CCleaner's registry mode, CleanMyPC, and countless others find hundreds of "problems" on a brand-new Windows installation. They're inventing issues to look useful — and they cause real damage. Microsoft explicitly warns against them. Professional IT never uses them to solve actual problems, because no real problem is solved by cleaning the registry.
  • "Your PC is infected" pop-ups are fake. A website cannot detect viruses on your computer. These pop-ups — especially those that play audio or lock your screen — are scams trying to get you to call a fake tech support number or install malware disguised as a fix.
  • Unsolicited "tech support" calls are always fake. Microsoft, Windows, your ISP, and your antivirus company do not call you to tell you your computer has a virus. Hang up. Full stop.
  • PC optimizer tools don't work. "SpeedUpMyPC," "Advanced SystemCare," and similar tools use the same scare tactics as registry cleaners. They slow systems down and add adware. Windows has its own built-in disk cleanup (cleanmgr.exe) that's more effective and safe.
  • Privacy "telemetry blockers" can break security. Tools that promise to disable Windows telemetry often touch low-level OS settings they shouldn't, and regularly disable actual security features in the process. Leave Windows privacy settings to the Settings app itself.

Chapter 06

Computer Maintenance

Occasional maintenance keeps Windows fast and clean. Here's what actually matters — done in the right order.

  • Uninstall programs you don't use. Go to Settings > Apps. Remove anything unfamiliar or unused — especially toolbars, "helpers," or expired antivirus software. Old, unused software is an attack surface.
  • Run the built-in disk cleanup. Search "cleanmgr" in Start, run as administrator, and check everything except Downloads. It can free 6GB+ by cleaning Windows component caches that no other tool can safely touch.
  • Run MalwareBytes for a checkup. MalwareBytes AdwCleaner removes adware and browser hijackers. MalwareBytes Anti-Malware does a broader scan. Both have free versions that work well. Review everything before agreeing to remove it.
  • Verify Windows Update and Firewall are working. Classic signs of infection damage: Windows Update fails, or the Firewall can't be turned on. If either is broken, that's a priority fix.
  • Update your browser and apps. Manually check: Chrome: Menu > Help > About. Firefox: Menu > Help > About. Make sure they're current.
  • Update drivers from the manufacturer. Go to your computer maker's website (Dell, Lenovo, HP, etc.) and download current drivers for your model. Outdated drivers cause mysterious crashes, slow performance, and compatibility issues.
  • Clean the vents. Heat kills hardware and throttles performance. Use compressed air on laptop vents once a year. Hold fans still while blowing — don't spin them fast or you'll damage the bearings.
Don't defrag an SSD. Windows knows to skip defrag on solid-state drives automatically. Never run third-party defraggers on SSDs — it wears them out with unnecessary writes.

Chapter 07

AI Threats in 2026 New

The threat landscape has fundamentally shifted. AI has made certain scams faster, cheaper, and far more convincing than anything that came before.

  • Voice cloning is real and cheap. Criminals can clone someone's voice from a few seconds of public audio. A distressed "family member" or "colleague" calling to ask for an urgent wire transfer, gift cards, or login credentials may not be who they sound like.
  • Real-time video deepfakes exist. Businesses have lost significant sums to fake "executive" video calls authorizing transactions. If something feels off about a video call — especially one making an unusual financial or access request — trust that feeling.
  • Establish a family safe word. Agree on a private code word in advance. Anyone who can't produce it in a claimed emergency call isn't who they claim to be. This takes 30 seconds to set up and costs nothing.
  • AI-written phishing looks professional. Perfect grammar, your real name, accurate company details — all possible now. Assess emails by where the link actually goes, not by how polished the writing is.
  • Always verify out-of-band. If an unusual request arrives by email, confirm it by phone. If it arrives by phone, confirm it by text or email. Never verify through the same channel the request arrived on.
For businesses: Implement a verbal confirmation protocol for any wire transfer, password reset, or unusual IT request regardless of how the request arrives. One extra phone call can prevent losses in the tens of thousands.

Chapter 08

If You Got Phished or Infected

Don't panic. Here's exactly what to do — in the right order.

  • Change the compromised password immediately from a different, trusted device. Enable MFA on that account if it isn't already on.
  • Report the phishing link. Right-click the link in the email — copy it without clicking. Report it to Google (google.com/safebrowsing/report_phish) and Microsoft (microsoft.com/wdsi/support/report-unsafe-site). This blocks it for others within minutes.
  • Check suspicious links at urlscan.io or VirusTotal before ever opening them. Paste the URL and get a safe preview and blacklist check. Free, instant, no account needed.
  • Got a spam text with a link? Forward it to 7726 (SPAM). Every major US carrier accepts this. It reports the sender to your carrier for investigation and blocking — no account needed.
  • Run MalwareBytes. If you think you downloaded something bad, run MalwareBytes Anti-Malware (free version). It finds what Windows Defender sometimes misses, especially adware and browser hijackers.
  • If it feels serious, call your IT provider. Ransomware, compromised business email, or suspected network intrusion are not DIY situations. The faster you call, the more options you have.
